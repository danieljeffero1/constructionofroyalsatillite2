Welcome to Your Satellite Project

This guide is designed for beginners, schools, hobbyists, and small organizations. Start with a receiving-only ground station, learn the systems, and then build a tabletop educational prototype.

Important distinction: A tabletop prototype is not a flight-ready satellite. A satellite intended for orbit requires specialist engineering, environmental testing, launch-provider approval, spectrum coordination, and applicable regulatory authorization.

Recommended project sequence

1. Learn and Receive

Explore satellite tracking and receive permitted signals before attempting to build hardware for orbit.

2. Build a Bench Prototype

Use a small frame, low-voltage electronics, a simulated payload, and test instruments.

3. Test Each Subsystem

Document power use, mass, heat, communication concepts, and failure conditions.

4. Research Flight Requirements

Consult qualified aerospace engineers and official regulatory and launch resources.

Build a Receiving-Only Ground Station

A receiving station lets you learn about satellite passes and radio signals without transmitting. Check signal compatibility, local rules, and the selected satellite's published receiving guidance before purchasing equipment.

1. Antenna

Select an antenna suited to the frequency, polarization, and satellite signals you intend to receive.

2. SDR or Receiver

A software-defined radio (SDR) can turn compatible radio signals into data for computer software.

3. Coax and Adapters

Use compatible connectors and suitable cable. Cable loss can reduce weak signals.

4. Computer

Use an existing computer where possible. Check operating-system and device compatibility.

5. Software

Research SatDump, Gpredict, SatNOGS, and GNU Radio. Each has different purposes and hardware requirements.

6. Tracking

Use published orbital elements and tracking tools to plan visible passes and learn about antenna pointing.

Safety, Radio Rules, and Launch Reality

Do not transmit without authorization. Receiving radio signals and transmitting radio signals are different activities. Spacecraft and ground-station transmissions may require licenses, frequency coordination, and other approvals. Amateur radio rules do not automatically authorize every satellite or frequency.
  • Never connect batteries or power supplies without checking voltage, polarity, current limits, and component ratings.
  • Use appropriate battery protection and never leave damaged or overheating batteries unattended.
  • Do not launch homemade hardware or attach equipment to a launch vehicle without the required provider acceptance and approvals.
  • Actual space missions can require substantial engineering, testing, integration, insurance, licensing, and launch expenses. A low-cost classroom prototype is not a low-cost orbital mission.
  • Before a real mission, seek qualified aerospace, spectrum, and regulatory advice.
